Methods Manual

What each technique in SLM Foundry actually does, the objective it optimises, where it is implemented, and how it fails. Assumes comfort with gradient descent and basic probability; no reinforcement-learning background required.

One distinction worth fixing early π_ref and π_old are different things and are easy to conflate. π_ref is fixed for an entire run and anchors the model to its starting behaviour. π_old is refreshed every batch and exists only to correct for the fact that samples were drawn from slightly stale parameters. In SLM Foundry π_ref is obtained by switching the LoRA adapters off — see below.

LoRA — Low-Rank Adaptation

Foundations

What it is

A method for fine-tuning a large weight matrix by training a small additive correction to it, leaving the original frozen. It is the reason fine-tuning a 1B-parameter model is possible on one consumer GPU.

The construction

For a frozen weight matrix W ∈ ℝ^(d×k), LoRA learns two thin matrices A ∈ ℝ^(r×k) and B ∈ ℝ^(d×r) with r ≪ min(d, k), and replaces the layer's output with:

h = W·x + (α / r) · B · A · x └──────┬──────┘ the only trained part

r is the rank — the bottleneck width. α is a scaling constant; the ratio α/r is what actually matters, which is why doubling r is usually paired with doubling α.

Why it works

The empirical claim behind LoRA is that the update a fine-tune needs is intrinsically low-rank, even though the weights themselves are not. Adapting a model to a new domain shifts behaviour along a small number of directions; r = 8–64 captures enough of them. You are not compressing the model, you are compressing the change to it.

The initialisation is load-bearing

A is Kaiming-initialised and B is set to zero, so B·A = 0 and training begins at exactly the base model's behaviour. Initialise B randomly and step zero is already a damaged model, with the first several hundred steps spent undoing the damage.

The trick this enables

Reference policy for free Because the adapter is purely additive and starts at zero, setting B·A = 0 again recovers the original weights exactly. So π_ref is just this model with the adapters switched off — no second copy in memory, and no possibility of drift between the reference you believe you have and the one you compute against. Every preference method here relies on it (trainers/lora.py :: adapters_disabled()).

Worked example

A 1.5B model with hidden size 1536. One attention projection is 1536 × 1536 ≈ 2.36M parameters. At r = 16:

A: 16 × 1536 = 24,576 B: 1536 × 16 = 24,576 ──────────────────── total 49,152 ≈ 2.1% of that matrix

Across all targeted projections this typically lands around 0.1–1% of total parameters trainable. The saved adapter is a few megabytes rather than several gigabytes, so a registry can hold hundreds of variants cheaply.

Practical notes

  • Which layers. Attention projections (q,k,v,o) plus the MLP (gate,up,down). Embeddings and the output head are deliberately excluded.
  • Rank. 8 for format-following, 16 default, 32–64 for a real domain shift. Higher rank overfits small datasets faster.
  • Precision. Adapter maths is kept in fp32 even under a half-precision base: the updates are small and low-rank, and fp16 accumulation eats them.
  • Learning rate. Roughly 10× a full fine-tune — 2e-4 rather than 2e-5 — because only a thin bottleneck is receiving the gradient.

Supervised fine-tuning

Foundations

Objective

Maximise the likelihood of the demonstrated answer given the prompt:

L_SFT(θ) = − (1 / Σ|y|) · Σ_examples Σ_t log π_θ( y_t | x, y_<t )

Plain cross-entropy over the answer's tokens. Everything interesting is in the two design choices below.

Choice 1 — mask the prompt

The sum runs over the tokens of y only. Include the prompt and the model spends most of its gradient learning to generate your instructions, which is capacity spent on something you will never ask it to do. This is the most common silent bug in a fine-tuning stack: nothing errors, the loss curve looks fine, and the model is measurably worse.

Choice 2 — normalise by token, not by sequence

Dividing by the total token count across the batch rather than averaging per-sequence losses. Under sequence-mean, a 4-token answer and a 400-token answer contribute equally, so each token of the short answer carries 100× the weight — which quietly turns your dataset into one dominated by its shortest examples.

Failure modes

SymptomCauseResponse
val_loss rises while loss falls Memorising the training set. Fewer epochs, lower rank, more data.
Loss plateaus immediately Learning rate too low, or LoRA matched no useful layers. Raise LR; check modules_replaced in the run log.
Outputs never stop Training examples lack a terminator. Ensure EOS is appended — it is, automatically, so suspect truncation instead.
Style copied, facts wrong Working as designed. SFT teaches form. Facts need retrieval or far more data.

Bradley–Terry reward models

Preference

The model of preference

Bradley–Terry (1952) is a model of pairwise comparison. It posits a latent quality score for each item, and says the probability one beats another is the logistic function of their difference:

P( y_w ≻ y_l | x ) = σ( r(x, y_w) − r(x, y_l) ) = exp r(x,y_w) ───────────────────────── exp r(x,y_w) + exp r(x,y_l)

Only differences are identified — adding a constant to every score changes nothing. So the absolute value of a reward is meaningless; only comparisons are.

Fitting it

Maximum likelihood over your observed preferences gives:

L_RM(φ) = − E [ w · log σ( r_φ(x, y_w) − r_φ(x, y_l) ) ]

w is the pair's margin weight, so a unanimous 5–0 reviewer split moves the scorer further than a contested 3–2.

Architecture

The policy backbone plus a scalar head, read at the last real token of the sequence. Mean-pooling instead would be gameable: every additional bland token drags the average toward whatever the model considers neutral, so padding a good answer with filler moves its score. Reading the final position forces a verdict on the completed response.

The failure mode to expect

Length bias

Human reviewers mildly prefer longer answers. A reward model fitted on their judgements learns “longer is better” as a shortcut, because it is a far easier function than “more helpful”. A policy optimised against it then discovers it can raise its score by padding — and the reward climbs while quality falls.

SLM Foundry reports length_bias_corr — the correlation between reward difference and length difference on held-out pairs — without being asked. Above about 0.4, your reward model is substantially a length detector.

Two other numbers, logged every step:

  • pair_acc — how often the scorer agrees with the humans. Stuck near 0.5 means the preferences carry no signal it can find.
  • reward_margin — how far apart it puts them. Very high accuracy and a huge margin usually means a shortcut was found.

Where it is used

Required by PPO. Optional elsewhere as an offline evaluator, or as the reward source for GRPO/GSPO. DPO does not need one — that is its central claim.

Implementation: src/foundry/trainers/reward.py, policy.py :: RewardModel

DPO — Direct Preference Optimization

Preference

The idea

Classical RLHF trains a reward model, then optimises the policy against it with RL. DPO observes that for the KL-constrained objective RLHF actually targets, the optimal policy has a closed form — so the reward model is an unnecessary intermediary that can be eliminated algebraically.

The derivation in three steps

The RLHF objective is:

max_θ E[ r(x,y) ] − β · KL( π_θ ‖ π_ref )

Its solution is known in closed form:

π*(y|x) = (1 / Z(x)) · π_ref(y|x) · exp( r(x,y) / β )

Rearranging for r expresses reward in terms of the policy:

r(x,y) = β · log ( π*(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) ) + β log Z(x)

Substituting that into the Bradley–Terry likelihood, the intractable partition function Z(x) is identical for both completions of a pair and cancels. What is left is a plain classification loss over pairs:

L_DPO(θ) = − log σ( β log π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x) − β log π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x) )
What this buys No reward model, no sampling, no rollout loop, no value function. Just two forward passes per pair, one of which is under a frozen reference. It is by a wide margin the most stable way to spend a fixed set of preference pairs, which is why it is the default here.

The implicit reward

DPO never trains a reward model but it defines one implicitly: r̂(x,y) = β log( π_θ(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) ). The logged metrics reward_chosen, reward_rejected and reward_margin are that quantity — useful for spotting saturation.

The characteristic failure

Likelihood displacement

The loss depends only on the difference of the two log-ratios. Nothing stops the optimiser from lowering log π_θ(y_w|x) — making your good answer less likely — as long as it lowers log π_θ(y_l|x) faster. Both probabilities fall, the loss improves, and the model gets worse at the thing you wanted.

This is why logp_chosen is logged every step. If it declines steadily: raise β, or set sft_weight > 0 to add an explicit NLL term on the chosen response (see RPO below).

Choosing β

βEffect
0.01–0.05Weak leash. Fits preferences hard, drifts, may degenerate.
0.1The standard default.
0.3–0.5Conservative. Use with noisy labels or a model you cannot afford to destabilise.

IPO, hinge, cDPO and RPO

Preference

Four modifications, all exposed as settings on a DPO run. Each addresses a specific weakness of the sigmoid loss.

IPO — Identity Preference Optimisation

DPO's sigmoid loss keeps improving as the log-ratio gap grows, so with clean, deterministic preferences it drives the gap toward infinity — overfitting to a preference that was only ever meant to indicate direction. IPO instead regresses the length-normalised gap onto a finite target:

L_IPO = ( h_θ − 1 / (2β) )² where h_θ = (1/|y_w|) log π_θ(y_w)/π_ref(y_w) − (1/|y_l|) log π_θ(y_l)/π_ref(y_l)

Use when your dataset is small, clean, or nearly deterministic. The length normalisation also makes it less length-sensitive than standard DPO.

Hinge (SLiC-style)

L_hinge = max( 0 , 1 − β · ( log-ratio gap ) )

Once a pair is separated by the margin it contributes no gradient at all. Cheaper and blunter; useful when most pairs are easy and you want the optimiser to spend its effort on the hard ones.

cDPO — conservative DPO (label smoothing)

Assumes a fraction ε of your preference labels are simply wrong:

L_cDPO = −(1−ε)·log σ(β·gap) − ε·log σ(−β·gap)

Bounds the loss a confidently-mislabelled pair can contribute, so one bad annotation cannot dominate a batch. Set label_smoothing to 0.1–0.2 for crowd-sourced data. Set it to your believed annotation error rate.

RPO — the auxiliary SFT term

L_RPO = L_DPO + λ · ( − log π_θ(y_w | x) )

The direct fix for likelihood displacement: an explicit reward for keeping the chosen response probable. sft_weight is λ. Start at 0.1; values above ~1.0 turn the run back into SFT.

The RLHF objective

RL

PPO, GRPO and GSPO all optimise the same thing. They differ only in how they estimate the gradient.

max_θ E_{x ~ D, y ~ π_θ(·|x)} [ r(x, y) ] − β · KL( π_θ ‖ π_ref ) └────────── get more reward ──────────┘ └─ without wandering off ─┘

Why the KL term is not optional

r is a proxy — a learned model, a judge, or a heuristic — and every proxy has a region where it is wrong. Unconstrained optimisation will find that region, because that is what optimisation does. This is Goodhart's law with a gradient attached. The KL term keeps the policy in the neighbourhood where the proxy was measured and is therefore still meaningful.

The KL estimator

Exact KL over a vocabulary is expensive, so it is estimated from samples. The naïve estimator −d where d = log π_ref − log π_θ is unbiased but has high variance and routinely goes negative on a batch — which would make a KL penalty occasionally pay the policy to move away from the reference. SLM Foundry uses Schulman's k3 estimator:

KL̂ = exp(d) − d − 1 ≥ 0 always, lower variance

Implemented as policy.py :: kl_k3 and shared by all three methods.

The credit-assignment problem

Reward arrives once, for a whole answer. The gradient needs a signal at every token. How each method bridges that gap is the entire difference between them:

  • PPO — learn a value function that predicts future reward at every position, and use it to distribute credit.
  • GRPO — sample a group of answers to the same prompt and use the group's mean as the baseline. No value function.
  • GSPO — same baseline, but stop pretending the correction is per-token and apply it once per sequence.

PPO — Proximal Policy Optimization

RL

The loop

for each iteration: 1. sample completions from π_θ for a batch of prompts 2. score them with the reward model 3. build per-token rewards (KL penalty + terminal score) 4. estimate advantages with GAE, using the critic V_ψ 5. take `ppo_epochs` clipped gradient steps on the same batch

Step 3 — turning one score into per-token rewards

The reward model emits one number for the whole completion. The standard construction, used here, spreads the KL penalty across every token and places the score at the final one:

r_t = − β · KL̂_t for t < T r_T = − β · KL̂_T + r_RM(x, y) at the last token

Step 4 — GAE

Generalised Advantage Estimation trades bias against variance with λ. With δ_t the one-step TD error:

δ_t = r_t + γ·V(s_{t+1}) − V(s_t) A_t = Σ_{l ≥ 0} (γλ)^l · δ_{t+l}

λ = 0 is pure TD — low variance, high bias. λ = 1 is Monte Carlo — unbiased, high variance. 0.95 is the usual compromise. Bootstrapping stops at the completion mask boundary, not the tensor edge; letting padded positions into the recursion drags every real advantage toward zero by however much padding the batch happened to contain.

Step 5 — the clipped surrogate

Several gradient steps are taken on one batch of samples, so after the first the data is off-policy. The importance ratio corrects for that, and clipping stops the correction from exploding:

ρ_t = π_θ(y_t) / π_old(y_t) L_pg = − E_t [ min( ρ_t · A_t , clip(ρ_t, 1−ε, 1+ε) · A_t ) ]

The min makes the objective pessimistic: it accepts the clipped value whenever clipping would have hurt, which removes the incentive to move a token's probability far in one update.

The critic

L_V = ½ · max( (V − R)² , (V_clipped − R)² ) where V_clipped = V_old + clip( V − V_old, −ε_v, +ε_v )

Clipping the value function too keeps the critic inside the same trust region as the policy. An unconstrained critic can move far enough in one epoch to invalidate the very advantages that epoch was computed from.

Adaptive KL control

A fixed β is either too loose early or too tight late, so the coefficient tracks a target KL and adjusts multiplicatively, clamped to ±20% per iteration. You specify “how far may this drift” rather than tuning a penalty by feel. Set target_kl = 0 to hold it fixed.

The diagnostic that matters most

explained_variance How much of the return variance the critic accounts for. Near 0 the value head has learned nothing and every advantage is mostly noise; negative means it is doing worse than predicting the mean, and your PPO run is not doing what it appears to be doing. Look here before interpreting any reward curve.

When to use it, honestly

PPO has the most knobs and the most ways to be subtly broken: a badly-fit critic, a stale reward model, a KL coefficient that is not actually binding. It is worth it when you need fine control over the reward–divergence trade-off and have the budget to tune it. For most preference data, DPO gets most of the benefit for a fraction of the operational risk.

Implementation: src/foundry/trainers/ppo.py

GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimization

RL

The idea

The critic exists only to provide a baseline — an estimate of “how good is this prompt normally?” — so the advantage measures whether an answer beat expectation. GRPO observes that if you sample G answers to the same prompt, their mean reward is already an unbiased estimate of exactly that. So delete the critic.

The advantage

for each prompt, sample y_1 … y_G ~ π_θ(·|x) A_i = ( r_i − mean(r_1..r_G) ) / std(r_1..r_G)

Note the advantage is a single scalar per completion — there is no per-token structure to it, which becomes the crux of GSPO's critique.

The objective

ρ_{i,t} = π_θ(y_{i,t}) / π_old(y_{i,t}) L = − (1 / Σ|y_i|) Σ_i Σ_t min( ρ_{i,t}·A_i , clip(ρ_{i,t}, 1±ε)·A_i ) + β · KL̂( π_θ ‖ π_ref )

What this costs and saves

PPOGRPO
Extra networkCritic, same size as policyNone
Memory~2× policy~1× policy
Samples per prompt1G (typically 4–16)
Can go wrong viaBad critic fitDegenerate groups

You trade critic memory for sampling compute. On tasks with a cheap reward function — a verifier, a test suite — that is usually an excellent trade.

Two subtleties

Degenerate groups

If all G samples score identically, the standard deviation is ~0 and dividing by it would manufacture an enormous gradient out of floating-point noise. Such groups contribute exactly zero and are counted in the logs. A few are normal; all of them means your reward source cannot distinguish the model's outputs, and no amount of tuning will help until that is fixed.

The std normalisation is a biased choice

Dividing by the group standard deviation stabilises early training, but it also up-weights prompts the policy already answers consistently — a prompt whose samples differ by a hair gets those hairs amplified to unit scale. Removing it (the “Dr. GRPO” correction, scale_by_std = false) drops that bias at some cost in stability.

GSPO — Group Sequence Policy Optimization

RL

The critique

The mismatch GSPO fixes The reward — and therefore the advantage A_i — is a property of the whole sequence. GRPO nonetheless applies a per-token importance correction to it. On a 500-token generation that means 500 independently noisy ratios multiplying a single shared reward signal. One token whose probability moved sharply can dominate the update, and per-token clipping cannot prevent it because it clips each token in isolation.

The fix

One ratio per sequence, normalised by length:

s_i = ( π_θ(y_i|x) / π_old(y_i|x) ) ^ (1/|y_i|) = exp( (1/|y_i|) · Σ_t [ log π_θ(y_{i,t}) − log π_old(y_{i,t}) ] ) L = − (1/N) Σ_i min( s_i·A_i , clip(s_i, 1−ε_lo, 1+ε_hi)·A_i )

One ratio, one clipping decision, one unit of credit per answer — matching the granularity of the correction to the granularity of the reward.

Why the clip ranges look wrong

ε ≈ 3e-4, not 0.2

s_i is a geometric mean over hundreds of per-token ratios. Averaging in log-space concentrates it extremely tightly around 1.0 — far more tightly than any individual token ratio. A clip range of 0.2 would therefore never bind, silently disabling clipping altogether.

GSPO's defaults are roughly three orders of magnitude tighter (clip_low ≈ 3e-4, clip_high ≈ 4e-4). That is the equivalent strictness, not a typo. If the run warns that nearly every sequence is being clipped, the learning rate is too high for the range — lower the rate rather than widening the clip.

When GSPO wins

  • Long generations. The variance problem scales with length; below ~100 tokens the two are close.
  • Off-policy passes. Sequence-level clipping tolerates mu > 1 far better, which is where the throughput gain lives. With mu = 1 every ratio is exactly 1.0 and GSPO reduces to a plain group-baseline policy gradient.
  • GRPO instability. Runs that collapse or spike without an obvious cause are the canonical symptom.

The trade-off

Sequence-level credit is coarser. Every token in a good answer is reinforced equally, including the mediocre ones. For long-form generation that coarseness costs less than the variance it removes; for short outputs where a single token genuinely carries the reward, GRPO's finer attribution can be better.

Implementation: src/foundry/trainers/group_rl.py (mode gspo) — deliberately the same file as GRPO, because they differ in one place.

RLAIF — reinforcement learning from AI feedback

Feedback

What it replaces

The labelling step, not the optimiser RLAIF substitutes a judge model for the humans who would otherwise sit in the review console. The preferences it produces are then consumed by DPO, GRPO or GSPO exactly as human preferences would be. “We used RLAIF” therefore says nothing about how the weights moved — which is why optimizer is a first-class, visible setting here rather than a hidden default.
prompts → sample K candidates → judge ranks them → preference pairs → optimiser

Why it works at all

The asymmetry between generating and evaluating. Producing an excellent answer is hard; recognising which of two answers is better is much easier, and a capable model can do it at a level that correlates usefully with human judgement — for tasks where quality is visible in the text. That qualification is the whole ballgame.

The three biases and their mitigations

1. Position bias

Judges systematically favour whichever response they read first — an effect large enough to flip a substantial share of verdicts. Every comparison is scored in both orders and averaged:

v₁ = judge(x, A, B) v₂ = judge(x, B, A) if v₁ and v₂ disagree → record a tie, margin 0, flag position_flip else → winner stands, margins averaged

Doubles the cost, and buys the position_flip_rate — the cheapest available measure of whether the judge is responding to content or to layout. Above ~25%, treat its labels as weak evidence.

2. Manufactured preferences

A judge forced to break a genuine tie invents a preference, and an invented preference is indistinguishable downstream from a real one — it is noise wearing a label. min_margin discards pairs the judge could barely separate. Ties are a first-class verdict, not a failure to decide.

3. Unfalsifiability

A judge nothing ever disagrees with is a system whose label quality has never been measured. human_spot_check routes a fraction of judged pairs into the human review queue, and the resulting overlap produces the agreement statistics below.

Verbosity and self-preference

Two biases with no cheap mitigation Judges prefer longer, more confident-sounding answers, and prefer text stylistically similar to their own output. Neither is corrected automatically. Watch completion_len during RLAIF runs: steadily growing answers with rising reward is the signature.

Constitutional AI, briefly

The related approach (Bai et al., 2022) has the model critique and revise its own output against written principles before any preference is collected. SLM Foundry's rubric — the weighted criteria the judge scores against — is the lightweight version of the same idea: an explicit, inspectable statement of what “better” means, rather than an unstated one.

The offline fallback

With no API key configured, the judge degrades to heuristic-v1: a mechanical scorer over four stated proxies — reference overlap, degenerate repetition, length plausibility and prompt echo. It is not an approximation of a model's taste and does not claim to be. It is labelled heuristic-v1 in every record it writes so nothing downstream can mistake it for one, and it exists so the pipeline remains runnable end to end.

Implementation: src/foundry/judge.py, trainers/rlaif.py

Reward functions

Feedback

Three sources, one signature — f(prompts, completions) → [float] — so PPO, GRPO and GSPO never learn which one they are using.

1. Learned — a reward model

r = σ( r_φ(x, y) / 4 ) squashed into (0,1)

Highest fidelity to what reviewers actually said. Its weakness is structural: as the policy improves it produces answers unlike anything the scorer was fitted on, and the score drifts from reality — the reason classical RLHF pipelines periodically refresh the reward model.

2. Mechanical — a verifier

Where the prompts carry a reference answer. Token-F1 with a bonus for an exact normalised match:

F1 = 2PR / (P + R) over token overlap with the reference exact = 1 if normalised strings match, else 0 r = min( 1, F1 · (1 − b) + exact · b ) b = 0.3

The exact-match bonus is not cosmetic. Under pure F1, an answer padded with every plausible response earns most of the credit, and a policy optimised against that learns to hedge exhaustively.

Free per rollout and impossible to game — but only available where answers are checkable. Chosen automatically when at least half the prompts carry references.

3. Judged — a model

The judge's weighted rubric score, normalised to 0–1 and cached on (prompt, completion). The cache matters: GRPO with G = 8 resamples the same prompt constantly and duplicate generations are common early in training, when the policy is still near-greedy. A judge outage returns 0.5, the neutral value — it contributes nothing to a group-relative advantage rather than pulling it somewhere false.

Why everything is normalised to ~[0,1]

Not tidiness — correctness KL coefficients, clip ranges and advantage scales are all tuned against an assumed reward magnitude. Swapping a source emitting logits in [−8, 8] for one emitting [0, 1] silently changes the effective strength of every other RL hyperparameter in the catalogue. Normalising at the boundary makes the settings portable across reward sources.

Blending

Sources can be combined by weight — for instance 0.7 × reward model + 0.3 × verifier, where the verifier anchors correctness and the reward model covers everything correctness does not.

Evaluation metrics

Measurement

Multiple choice — by likelihood, not generation

Given choices and an answer, each option is scored under the model and the highest wins:

pick = argmax_c (1/|c|) · Σ_t log π_θ( c_t | x, c_<t )

Length-normalised. Raw summed log-probability always prefers the shortest option, turning multiple choice into a length contest. And ranking rather than generating means a model that knows the answer but phrases it loosely is not marked wrong — that would measure formatting compliance, not knowledge.

Open generation

  • Exact match — after normalising case and whitespace. Brittle, but unambiguous where it applies.
  • Token F1 — harmonic mean of precision and recall over token overlap. Forgiving of phrasing, blind to word order and negation.
  • Judge score — a model grading against a rubric. Captures what the other two cannot; carries every judge bias described above.

Two metrics reported unprompted

degenerate_rate The share of outputs that collapsed — one token repeated, or a short phrase looping. Detected by token-type ratio below 0.25 or any n-gram exceeding 40% of all n-grams. This and runaway length are the two most common fine-tuning failures, and both leave the task metric looking merely unremarkable rather than obviously broken.

Head-to-head comparison

Absolute scores drift with prompt formatting and sampling settings. “Which of these two answers is better” is both more stable and closer to the question a stakeholder is actually asking. Reported with a Wilson score interval rather than a bare proportion:

p̂ + z²/2n ± z·√( p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/4n² ) CI₉₅ = ────────────────────────────────────────────── 1 + z²/n

Wilson rather than the normal approximation because it stays inside [0,1] near the extremes, where the normal approximation produces impossible bounds. Without an interval, a 6–4 result on ten prompts reads as a win; with one, it reads as the coin flip it is.

The statistics of review

Measurement

Human review is a sampling procedure, and treating it as one is what makes the resulting numbers mean anything.

Propensity, and why it is recorded

The queue deliberately favours informative items — high model uncertainty, items still short of their annotation target. That is good for label efficiency and fatal for naïve estimation: any rate computed from the reviewed set is biased by the sampling, and by an unknown amount unless the sampling probability is known.

So assignment is sampled, not sorted:

score_i = ( need_i · 0.5 + uncertainty_i + priority_i · 0.25 ) / τ p_i = softmax(score)_i ← stored on the assignment row

A deterministic “hardest first” queue is a policy with an unknown propensity. Softmax sampling makes p_i well defined and non-zero for everything in the pool.

Inverse-propensity weighting

w_i = clip( p_uniform / p_i , 0.2 , 5.0 ) p_uniform = 1/40

Expressed relative to what a uniform queue would have given, so an unbiased sampler yields w = 1. The clip is not cosmetic: an item sampled with p = 0.001 would otherwise arrive carrying forty times its neighbours' weight and decide the update by itself.

Consensus and the training margin

agreement = votes for the plurality choice / total votes margin = max( 0, 2·agreement − 1 ) · mean(confidence)

Unanimous and confident → 1.0. A 3–2 split → 0.2 before the confidence discount. That margin becomes the DPO pair weight, so a contested item genuinely moves the policy less far than a decisive one. Below 0.6 agreement the item is routed to a lead for adjudication rather than being settled by majority-of-two.

Agreement: raw, and corrected

On a two-way choice, two annotators guessing randomly agree 50% of the time. Raw agreement of 60% therefore looks respectable and means very little. Cohen's kappa subtracts the chance baseline:

κ = ( p_o − p_e ) / ( 1 − p_e ) p_o = observed agreement p_e = agreement expected from the two label distributions alone
κReadingAction
< 0.2Barely above chanceDo not train on these labels.
0.2 – 0.4WeakUsable with a large human spot-check.
0.4 – 0.6ModerateReasonable for scaling labelling.
> 0.6StrongThe judge tracks your reviewers.

Low kappa between humans is usually not carelessness — it is a sign the question is underspecified. Sharpen the rubric before blaming the annotators.
